Research plan

Provably safe automation for premium audit.

Our research proposal to the U.S. National Science Foundation's SBIR program asks a question the insurance industry hasn't answered yet. The answer is what lets Penny take on more work without asking anyone to take it on faith.

How does an AI system prove which audit decisions it can make on its own, when mistakes are measured in dollars?

Who benefits

Accurate to the penny, for everyone the audit touches.

An audit done right is the same audit for the business paying it and the insurer collecting it. That is why the research is worth doing for all three.

Small businesses

  • No surprise bills built on a misread payroll report or the wrong class code
  • Every line explained in plain language, with the rule behind it
  • A fast, fair way to fix mistakes without hiring a consultant
  • Fewer document requests, because Penny asks for the right records the first time

Large employers

  • Consistent treatment across many locations, states and policy years
  • Multi-state payroll allocated the same way every time, with an audit trail
  • Disputes resolved on evidence instead of negotiation
  • Audits that finish on time, so budgets and renewals are not held hostage

Insurers and auditors

  • A dollar-based guarantee for any audit type they automate
  • Auditors spend their time on judgment, not chasing documents
  • Fewer disputes reach the audit team, and the rest arrive organized
  • Premiums that accurately fund benefits for injured workers
The gap

Today's methods offer guarantees that premium audit breaks.

Conformal prediction, conformal risk control and learning-to-defer can tell a system when to hand a decision to a person. They rely on assumptions that don't hold in audit.

The approach

Risk-controlled learning to defer, built for audit.

Penny decides, audit type by audit type, which decisions it may finalize on its own. It guarantees, at a confidence level the carrier sets, that dollar-weighted premium error on automated files stays below a target. Every auditor correction feeds both learning and the guarantee.

Phase I objectives

Four objectives, one core question.

Objective 1 · the core

Dollar-weighted risk control

A rule that chooses which files to auto-clear while bounding expected premium error on cleared files below a target, such as 1% of premium.

Success: the bound holds on held-out audits while at least 40% of routine files clear automatically.
Objective 2

Learning from few, noisy labels

Pooling evidence across audit types and carriers, and modeling disagreement between auditors on a double-reviewed sample.

Success: valid control with 50% fewer labels per audit type than a no-pooling baseline.
Objective 3

Staying valid as rules change

Drift detection and reweighted calibration when manuals update or a new carrier joins, falling back to human review when validity can't be certified.

Success: no loss of error control across a simulated annual manual change and a held-out carrier.
Objective 4

The inputs it governs

Rule-grounded classification across NCCI and California WCIRB rules, plus payroll reconciliation across 941s, state wage reports and registers.

Success: 90% agreement with final auditor codes, calibration error at or below 5%, 98% of payroll dollars traced to source.
The hard problems

What actually has to be solved.

Each problem below has a plain-language summary. Open one to see the technical detail, or skip them all and keep the picture: these are the places where "AI for audit" usually breaks, and where this research puts its effort.

1. A few errors carry most of the moneyAudit mistakes are not evenly sized. One missed uninsured subcontractor can cost more than a hundred small corrections, so a guarantee on average error says little.+

Why it is hard

Conformal risk control gives finite-sample guarantees for losses that are bounded and reasonably well behaved. Dollar-weighted premium error is heavy-tailed: most files contribute cents, a few contribute thousands. A bound on the mean is dominated by rare events the calibration set may never have seen.

What we will try

  • Cap the per-file loss at a policy-level ceiling and control the capped risk, while a separate tail detector routes high-exposure signals (large 1099 payments with no certificate, large payroll swings) to a person regardless of the model's confidence
  • Control tail risk directly with a conditional value-at-risk style objective, not only the mean
  • Evaluate on the dollar distribution, not accuracy, and report the realized tail separately

How we will know

On held-out audits the realized dollar error on auto-cleared files stays under the target at the stated confidence, including in the worst 5% of files. If the tail breaks the bound, the method fails, and we say so.

2. Not enough examples where you need themA guarantee needs calibration data. A single carrier may have only a dozen corrections for a given class and state.+

Why it is hard

Finite-sample conformal guarantees have a resolution floor: with n calibration points you cannot certify a miss rate finer than about 1 / (n + 1). Certifying a 1% rate needs on the order of 99 clean, exchangeable examples for that group. Split the book by class, state and carrier, and most groups are far smaller.

What we will try

  • Partial pooling across related groups (an empirical Bayes or hierarchical prior), so a new carrier starts from the industry pattern and moves toward its own data as corrections arrive
  • Group-conditional calibration where groups are large enough, pooled calibration where they are not, with the choice made by a rule, not by hand
  • Report the sample size behind every certification so a carrier can see how much evidence supports it

How we will know

Objective 2's target: valid control with half the labels a no-pooling baseline needs, measured on held-out groups.

3. The experts do not always agreeTwo good auditors can classify the same employee differently. If the "right answer" is uncertain, so is any guarantee built on it.+

Why it is hard

Risk control assumes the labels are the truth. In audit, a label is an expert judgment under time pressure. Disagreement between auditors sets a floor under any system's measurable error, and systematic bias in one carrier's labels would be learned as if it were correct.

What we will try

  • Double-review a sample of audits to measure agreement (a kappa statistic by class and state) and model each auditor's reliability with a Dawid and Skene style estimator
  • Treat labels as noisy observations of a latent truth when calibrating, and widen the guarantee where disagreement is high
  • Surface cases where Penny and the auditor disagree as review items, not as training data, until a second opinion settles them

How we will know

Calibration remains valid on the double-reviewed subset, and the disagreement floor is published alongside the error bound.

4. The rules change every yearClassification manuals update, rates change, a new carrier joins with a different book. A guarantee calibrated last year may not hold this year.+

Why it is hard

Conformal validity rests on exchangeability: tomorrow's files look like the calibration files. Rule changes, portfolio shifts and new states break that. Weighted conformal methods handle shift in the inputs if you can estimate how the distribution moved, which is itself uncertain. No method can promise validity under arbitrary, unmeasured change.

What we will try

  • Pin every run to the rule set in force for the policy's effective date, so a manual update is a known event, not a surprise
  • Detect drift in the inputs and in the correction rate; when it trips, recalibrate on recent data or fall back to human review for the affected group
  • Weight calibration data toward the current distribution where the shift can be estimated, and widen bounds where it cannot

How we will know

Error control holds across a simulated annual manual change and on a carrier held out entirely from calibration. Where it does not, the system defers rather than guesses.

5. Automation hides its own mistakesOnce Penny clears a file without review, nobody checks it. The data you need to keep the guarantee honest stops arriving.+

Why it is hard

This is the quiet failure in most automation programs. Labels only come from files a person reviewed, so auto-cleared files are missing not at random. Calibration drifts toward the files that were hard enough to review, and the guarantee silently stops describing the files it covers.

What we will try

  • Keep a random, uniformly sampled review stream inside every automated category: this is the Supervised stage, and it never goes to zero
  • Use the sampled reviews to re-certify the bound on a schedule, and shrink automation automatically if the sample shows drift
  • Treat the sampling rate as a tunable cost: the carrier chooses how much review to spend for how tight a guarantee

How we will know

Calibration computed from the sampled stream matches calibration on a fully reviewed holdout within the stated confidence.

6. Reading the documents is still hardPayroll registers, 941s and state wage reports describe the same money in different ways, often as scans. Every number Penny proposes has to trace to a source.+

Why it is hard

Periods misalign (calendar quarters versus policy periods), categories differ (gross wages versus taxable wages), and documents are incomplete or poorly scanned. Extraction errors propagate straight into premium. Language models read documents well and estimate their own uncertainty poorly.

What we will try

  • Treat reconciliation as constraint satisfaction: quarterly totals must sum, employee-level pay must roll up, and any violation becomes an explicit flag with a dollar size
  • Carry uncertainty from extraction through to premium so the risk controller can see it, rather than letting a confident wrong number pass
  • Prefer structured sources (payroll exports, filed 941s) over free text when both exist

How we will know

Objective 4's targets: 98% of audited payroll dollars traced to source and 90% recall on material discrepancies.

7. Citing the rule, and meaning itPenny explains each decision with the rule behind it. A citation that looks right but does not actually support the decision is worse than none.+

Why it is hard

Applying classification rules is close to statutory reasoning, where research shows language models still struggle: exceptions, cross-references and jurisdiction-specific definitions. Models also produce fluent but unfaithful citations. The right answer often depends on a fact that is simply not in the documents.

What we will try

  • Retrieve from versioned rule text and require the cited passage to entail the decision, checked by a second pass and by expert review on a sample
  • Abstain explicitly when a required fact is missing, and turn the gap into a document request rather than a guess
  • Score faithfulness separately from accuracy, because a correct code with a wrong citation is still a defect

How we will know

Experts judge the cited rule correct in at least 90% of sampled decisions, and the abstention rate falls as collection improves.

8. Same answer every timeCarriers, businesses and regulators need to be able to re-run a result and get the same number. Modern AI models are not naturally deterministic, and vendors retire them.+

Why it is hard

Language model outputs vary run to run unless decoding is pinned, and hosted models change or disappear on the vendor's schedule. A guarantee attached to one model version does not transfer automatically to the next.

What we will try

  • Pin model version, decoding settings and rule version on every run, and store enough to reproduce it
  • Treat a model change like a rule change: re-certify before it governs anything automated
  • Keep the risk controller independent of any one model, so the guarantee survives a model swap

How we will know

The consistency metric: the share of reruns that reproduce the original result, with a target of 100%.

9. People will test the systemSome insureds will submit documents designed to lower a bill. Some will simply make mistakes. Penny has to tell the difference without treating everyone as a suspect.+

Why it is hard

Any system that lets a party add evidence creates an incentive to add the right evidence. Classical risk control does not model an adversary. At the same time, most inconsistencies are honest errors, and a hostile posture would undo the trust that makes the dispute portal work.

What we will try

  • Cross-check every uploaded document against independent sources (filed 941s, state wage reports, certificate verification) and flag inconsistencies with their dollar impact
  • Route material inconsistencies to a person, never to automatic acceptance
  • Monitor whether dispute outcomes differ by business size or segment, so fairness is measured rather than assumed

How we will know

Synthetic adversarial files are caught at the target rate, and dollar error does not differ materially across business size bands.

A risk budget, not an accuracy score

How much evidence does a guarantee need?

Instead of asking "how accurate is the model," the right question is "how much review am I willing to spend for how tight a guarantee." Try it: pick the miss rate you would accept on automated files and see the minimum number of confirmed corrections needed before any certification is even possible.

This is the floor from the mathematics of conformal methods: with n confirmed corrections you cannot certify a miss rate finer than about 1 / (n + 1). Real requirements are higher once dollar weighting, label noise and drift are accounted for, which is exactly what Objectives 1 to 3 study.

Beyond premium audit

Any expert decision measured in dollars.

Premium audit is the first use because it is bounded, regulated and rich in expert labels. The method is general: wherever a professional reviews documents, applies rules and signs off on an amount, the same question applies. Which decisions can the machine make alone, and how much is at risk if it is wrong?

Insurance

Claims adjudication

Pay low-complexity claims straight through with a bound on reserve dollars at risk, while adjusters handle the rest. The error that matters is dollars misallocated, not claims misrouted.

Insurance

Underwriting intake

Straight-through processing for small commercial submissions, with a guarantee on expected loss cost error, certified on the underwriters' own corrections.

Insurance

Premium fraud and leakage

The reconciliation engine that traces payroll to source doubles as a leakage detector, with the tail detector routing large exposures to investigators.

Insurance

Regulatory reporting

Statistical reporting to rating bureaus depends on the same class and payroll data. Accurate audits improve the rates the whole market pays.

Finance and government

Tax examination and lending

Tax authorities selecting returns for exam, and lenders verifying income from the same payroll records, face the same dollar-weighted, expert-labeled problem.

Any industry

Invoice, medical coding and procurement audits

Billing codes against clinical notes, invoices against contracts, expenses against policy. Each is rule-bound review with a cost in dollars and a human who signs.

Go deeper: what transfers and what does notThe controller and the evaluation framework transfer. The document models and rule sets are domain-specific.+

Transfers

  • Dollar-weighted (cost-sensitive) risk control with tail handling
  • Partial pooling across small groups with per-group certification
  • The sampled-review design that keeps calibration honest after automation
  • Version pinning and re-certification on rule or model change
  • Evaluation on realized dollar error, with the disagreement floor published

Does not transfer

  • The classification rule corpus and its versioning (NCCI, independent bureaus)
  • Document reconciliation constraints specific to payroll and tax filings
  • The dispute workflow and who holds appeal rights

Why insurance first

Volume is fixed by regulation, labels come from licensed experts, and the cost of each error is literally written on the bill. Few domains make the research question this measurable.

What it would change

From trusting a percentage to budgeting a risk.

For carriers and regulators

Automation becomes something you can set a budget for and inspect, the way you set reserves. "The model is 95% accurate" is replaced by "automated files carry at most this much premium error, at this confidence, on our own data."

For businesses

The same guarantee protects the business: automated decisions are bounded in how wrong they can be in dollars, every decision shows its evidence, and a person is still reachable.

For auditors

The job shifts toward judgment: exceptions, disputes and the sampled reviews that keep the guarantee honest. The repetitive collection and arithmetic go away.

For AI in regulated work

A template for safe automation that does not depend on trusting a vendor's benchmark: certify on your own corrections, publish the bound, keep a human in the loop where the bound cannot be certified.

What a skeptic should ask

Fair questions, honest answers.

Can you really guarantee anything with so little data?+

Not for every audit type. The guarantee is per group, and groups with too few confirmed corrections stay in human review. That is the design, not a limitation we hide. The research question is how far partial pooling can safely lower the evidence needed.

What if the rules change and the model is wrong for months?+

Rule changes are pinned to effective dates, so they are known events that trigger re-certification. Unknown shifts are caught by drift monitoring and the sampled review stream, which shrinks automation automatically. Between those, there is a window of risk, and the bound's confidence level is how a carrier chooses its size.

Isn't a dollar bound just an accuracy bound with extra steps?+

No. Accuracy treats a $40 error and a $40,000 error the same. A dollar bound forces the system to route high-exposure files to a person even when the model is confident, which is the opposite of what an accuracy-optimized system does.

Who checks the checker?+

Every run is reproducible, every bound records the evidence behind it, and the benchmark and methods are intended for publication. A carrier's own auditors and a regulator can re-run any decision and recompute any bound.

What would make you conclude the approach failed?+

If realized dollar error on auto-cleared files exceeds the certified bound on held-out data, or if the sampled review stream cannot reproduce the calibration, the method has failed for that setting. Phase I is designed to find that out before anything is automated in production.
